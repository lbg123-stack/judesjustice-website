Health and hospital

Practical things to know about hospital stays, specialist treatment and planning ahead.

Going into hospital

  • Take a hospital passport with the essentials: how the person communicates, their medication, epilepsy plan, feeding plan and what distresses them.
  • Ask whether the hospital has a learning disability liaison nurse, and ask them to be involved from the start.
  • You can ask to stay with the person. You know them best, and staff may not recognise their signs of pain or distress.
  • On a neurology ward, ask who the lead consultant is, what the plan is for today, and what would make staff call you.
  • Before discharge, ask for everything agreed to be written down: medication changes, follow-up appointments and any new equipment or support.

Oxygen at home

  • Home oxygen, including oxygen concentrators, is prescribed by the person's NHS team, who arrange the supplier.
  • Everyone who supports the person should be trained to use it. Add it to the training record.
  • Tell your home insurer, and plan ahead before travelling, including for holidays and flights.

Ketogenic diet for epilepsy

A ketogenic diet is a medical treatment for some people with drug-resistant epilepsy. It must be run by a specialist dietitian, including for people who are tube fed.

Growing up with a shortened life expectancy

Some young people with PMLD are told in childhood that they may not live long. Many are still here as adults, and thriving, but the services built around them as children often stop at 18 or 21, and adult services have not planned for them.

  • Ask for transition planning well before 18: health, social care and education together.
  • Ask which adult consultant, nurse and therapists will take over, and when.
  • Make sure benefits move over in time (see Benefits from 16), and that guardianship is in place from 16.
  • Keep a record of every gap in support in My file. It is evidence if you need to complain.

Hospice and palliative care

Palliative care is about living as well as possible, not only about the end of life. It can run alongside other treatment for many years.

CHAS (Children's Hospices Across Scotland)

Supports children with life-shortening conditions, from before birth up to age 21, and their whole family, in hospice, at home, in the community or in hospital.

Website
chas.org.uk

Together for Short Lives

UK charity for families caring for a child with a life-shortening or life-threatening condition, including support with moving to adult services.

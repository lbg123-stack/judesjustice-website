Training

Training for personal assistants (PAs), families and carers supporting someone with PMLD, and a simple record to keep track of who has done what.

Start with the person

General courses are not enough on their own. Ask the person's doctor, epilepsy nurse, speech and language therapist or physiotherapist for their own written plans, and make sure everyone who supports them has read them.

Emergency epilepsy medication

Some people with PMLD are prescribed emergency medication, such as buccal or nasal midazolam, to stop a prolonged seizure. Anyone who may need to give it should be trained.

Epilepsy Scotland: Understanding epilepsy and administration of Midazolam

A full-day course for people caring for someone with epilepsy who has been prescribed emergency midazolam. It covers epilepsy and best-practice techniques for giving buccal and nasal midazolam. Suitable for families, care providers and employers.

Epilepsy Scotland also runs a course on rectal paraldehyde and rectal diazepam.

Always follow the person's own plan

Training teaches the technique. The dose, when to give it and when to call 999 must come from the person's own emergency medication plan, written by their doctor or epilepsy nurse.

Clinical care tasks

Mencap notes that a growing number of people with PMLD are "technology dependent", needing things like oxygen, tube feeding or suctioning. Much of this care would usually be done by nurses. Families and PAs often learn to do it at home. This training is specific to the person and usually comes from their NHS team, who should check and sign off each person as competent before they do it alone.

Care taskWho to ask for training
PEG, gastrostomy and other tube feedingThe person's dietitian or nutrition nurse
Suction (mouth, nose or tracheostomy)The person's community nursing or respiratory team
Ventilation, including overnight ventilationThe person's ventilation team
Tracheostomy careThe person's tracheostomy or community nursing team
OxygenThe person's respiratory team
Postural care, positioning and physiotherapyThe person's physiotherapist or occupational therapist
Giving medicationThe person's GP, pharmacist or community nurse

Ask for it in writing

Ask the NHS team to write down who has been trained, in what, and when they need a refresher. Add it to the training record below. If nobody will train the people who support the person, that is a gap in their care, and you can complain using our complaint letter.

Communication and Intensive Interaction

Intensive Interaction is an approach for connecting with people who do not use speech, by following their lead and responding to their sounds, movements and expressions. Training helps PAs and families build real two-way communication.

Eating, drinking and swallowing

Many people with PMLD have a modified diet or thickened drinks. Their speech and language therapist sets the right levels. Everyone who helps them eat and drink should know those levels and how to prepare food safely.

Insurance for Option 1 employers

If you employ PAs with a Direct Payment (Option 1), you are their employer. Insurance matters most when PAs carry out clinical tasks such as tube feeding, suction or giving emergency medication.

Employers' liability insurance

  • The Employers' Liability (Compulsory Insurance) Act 1969 requires employers carrying on a business to insure against injury or disease their employees suffer at work.
  • HSE's guidance calls domestic employment "one difficult area": if you employ someone who works only for you, "you may be required to take out insurance to protect them".
  • HSE says cover must be at least £5 million, and you must display the certificate where employees can read it.
  • The law does not require insurance for an employee who is a close relative of the employer, such as a parent, child, brother or sister (section 2(2)(a)).
  • Your Direct Payment agreement may also require insurance. Check it, and ask the council whether the cost is included in the budget.

Before PAs do clinical tasks

  • Tell your insurer, in writing, exactly which tasks PAs will do, for example PEG feeding, suction, ventilation or buccal midazolam.
  • Ask them to confirm in writing that these tasks are covered, and what training is needed for cover to apply.
  • Keep the training record below up to date. Your insurer may ask for it after an incident.
  • If the insurer will not cover a task, tell the council and the person's NHS team straight away. The person still needs that care.
